LEARN • Week 1 core review

1. What the heart has to be able to do

AutomaticityPacemaker cells can initiate an electrical impulse without another nerve having to trigger every beat.
ExcitabilityCardiac cells can respond to a chemical, mechanical or electrical stimulus.
ConductivityCardiac cells can receive an electrical impulse and pass it to adjacent cells.
ContractilityMyocardial cells can shorten and generate force after electrical activation.
RefractorinessAfter depolarization, a cell needs recovery time before it can respond normally again.
Pacemaker hierarchySA node about 60–100/min → AV junction about 40–60/min → Purkinje/ventricular backup about 20–40/min.

2. Autonomic effects you need to understand

Sympathetic activity generally increases heart rate, AV conduction and myocardial contractile force. Parasympathetic/vagal activity generally slows the heart and AV-nodal conduction.

ChronotropyChange in heart rate.
InotropyChange in myocardial contractile force.
DromotropyChange in conduction speed, especially through the AV junction.
Key clinical ideaThe autonomic nervous system modifies cardiac behaviour; it does not need to trigger every normal heartbeat.

3. Depolarization and repolarization

Depolarization is electrical activation. Repolarization is electrical recovery. The refractory periods matter because a cell cannot respond normally to every stimulus at every point in the cardiac cycle.

The ECG is an electrical recording. It does not directly prove effective mechanical contraction, a pulse or adequate cardiac output.

4. Normal conduction pathway

SA node → atrial myocardium → AV node/junction → Bundle of His → right & left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres → ventricular myocardium.

The AV node intentionally delays conduction. That delay gives the atria time to finish emptying into the ventricles before ventricular contraction.

5. ECG paper

Horizontal axis = time1 small box = 0.04 s. 1 large box = 0.20 s. 5 large boxes = 1 second. 30 large boxes = 6 seconds.
Vertical axis = voltageWave amplitude is displayed vertically. Current moving toward a positive electrode tends to create a positive/upward deflection.

Sinus family

Normal sinus rhythm60–100/min, regular, sinus P before every QRS, constant PR 0.12–0.20 s, narrow QRS.
Sinus bradycardiaSame sinus pattern but rate below 60. Clinical importance depends on perfusion and symptoms.
Sinus tachycardiaSinus origin with rate above 100. Ask what demand or stress is driving the SA node.
Sinus arrhythmiaSinus P waves remain consistent while the R–R interval varies, commonly with respiration.
SA exit blockImpulse is generated but does not successfully leave the SA node; pause tends to match a multiple of the basic P–P cycle.
Sinus arrestSA node fails to generate the expected impulse; the pause does not neatly match a multiple of the previous P–P interval.

Junctional family

Junctional impulses can travel down to the ventricles and backward toward the atria, so P waves may be inverted, buried in the QRS, occur after the QRS, or be difficult to see.

  • PJC: premature junctional complex.
  • Junctional escape beat: late protective beat after a higher pacemaker fails.
  • Junctional escape rhythm: several sequential junctional beats, usually around 40–60/min.
  • Accelerated junctional: around 60–100/min.
  • Junctional tachycardia: above 100/min.

Atrial rhythms

PACPremature atrial focus. P morphology may differ or be buried in the preceding T wave; QRS is usually narrow.
Atrial fibrillationNo organized P waves; irregularly irregular ventricular rhythm; fibrillatory baseline. Rapid AF = ventricular rate above 100.
Atrial flutterOrganized rapid atrial re-entry, commonly ~250–350/min. 2:1 conduction often gives ~150/min ventricular rate.
WAP vs MATBoth have at least 3 P-wave morphologies. WAP is under 100/min; MAT is 100/min or faster.

SVT, AVNRT, AVRT and WPW

AVNRT uses a re-entry circuit within/around the AV node. AVRT uses an accessory pathway outside the AV node. Classic manifest WPW pre-excitation produces a short PR interval, delta wave and widened QRS.

AV blocks

1st degree AV blockEvery P conducts, but PR is prolonged beyond 0.20 s and remains constant.
Mobitz I / WenckebachPR progressively lengthens until one P is not followed by a QRS; then the cycle resets.
Mobitz IIConducted beats have a constant PR, with intermittent non-conducted P waves/dropped QRS complexes.
3rd degree AV blockComplete AV dissociation: atria and ventricles run independently because atrial impulses do not conduct to the ventricles.
Do not confuse complete heart block with PEA. PEA means organized electrical activity without a palpable pulse. That requires a clinical pulse/perfusion assessment.
RECALL • High-yield discrimination
Sinus exit block vs sinus arrest — what is the timing clue?
Exit block tends to create a pause that is a multiple of the baseline P–P interval. Sinus arrest creates an unpredictable pause that is not a clean multiple.
AF vs atrial flutter — what is the basic visual distinction?
AF has no organized P waves and is irregularly irregular. Flutter has organized rapid atrial activity, classically sawtooth flutter waves; ventricular regularity depends on the conduction pattern.
Mobitz I vs Mobitz II?
Mobitz I progressively lengthens the PR before the dropped beat. Mobitz II has constant PR intervals on conducted beats with intermittent dropped QRS complexes.
What are the classic manifest WPW features?
Short PR interval, delta wave and widened QRS.
What makes PEA different from VF/VT?
PEA is organized electrical activity without a pulse and is non-shockable. Pulseless VF/VT are shockable cardiac-arrest rhythms.
