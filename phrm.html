LEARN • SGA directive review

What this lecture wants you to know

The lecture introduces the Supraglottic Airway Medical Directive, the I-GEL device, ALS PCS wording and the Companion Document. The exam-relevant part is not merely “what is an SGA?” It is knowing the directive structure and the wording that controls when/how it is used.

PurposeAn SGA provides a ventilation route above the glottic opening when a patient cannot adequately maintain/protect the airway.
I-GELThe Week 1 lecture uses the I-GEL as the primary device example.
Attempt limitThe lecture highlights a maximum of 2 attempts. Confirm exact wording in the current ALS PCS/Companion Document.
ConfirmationPlacement must be confirmed using the methods required by the standards; effective ventilation and capnography are key clinical tools.

Ventilation after an advanced airway

  • Chest compressions can continue continuously once an advanced airway is appropriately established in cardiac arrest.
  • The lecture lists adults at approximately 1 breath every 6 seconds.
  • The lecture lists pediatrics at approximately 1 breath every 3 seconds.
  • Avoid excessive ventilation; deliver enough volume to produce visible chest rise.
Test strategy: exact wording, contraindications, attempt limits, required confirmation and considerations are high-yield. Read the actual directive alongside this explanation.
RECALL • SGA checks
How many SGA attempts does the lecture highlight?
Maximum of 2 attempts — verify the precise directive wording in the current ALS PCS.
Why is capnography useful after SGA placement?
It contributes to confirmation/ongoing assessment of ventilation and can help detect displacement or changes in perfusion during resuscitation.
Adult ventilation rate highlighted by the lecture with an advanced airway during CPR?
About 1 breath every 6 seconds.

LEARN • Cardiac arrest framework

1. Separate electrical rhythm from pulse status

In arrest care, the monitor does not tell you everything. An organized-looking rhythm may still produce no pulse.

Ventricular fibrillationChaotic ventricular electrical activity with no effective organized ventricular contraction. In cardiac arrest, VF is shockable.
Pulseless VTVentricular tachycardia with no pulse. In cardiac arrest, pulseless VT is shockable.
AsystoleAbsence of meaningful ventricular electrical activity. Non-shockable.
PEAOrganized electrical activity without a palpable pulse. It is a clinical state, not one specific ECG pattern. Non-shockable.
Simple arrest rule: pulseless VF/VT = shockable. Asystole + PEA = non-shockable. VT with a pulse is not cardiac arrest and is managed differently.

2. CPR and ventilation updates highlighted in class

  • Ventilate only until visible chest rise; each breath is delivered over about 1 second.
  • Avoid hyperventilation because it can worsen hemodynamics and increase gastric insufflation/aspiration risk.
  • The lecture emphasizes a two-handed mask seal with one rescuer focused on the airway where staffing allows.
  • The lecture notes updated preferred infant compression techniques instead of the traditional two-finger method.
  • For choking in adults/children, the lecture notes alternating back blows and abdominal thrusts.

3. Pediatric symptomatic bradycardia

The lecture emphasizes HR < 60/min with cardiopulmonary compromise, rapid assessment of the cause, and optimization of airway, oxygenation and ventilation. If major bradycardia with compromise persists despite effective oxygenation/ventilation, CPR is initiated.

4. Reversible causes

Do not memorize PEA as “just a rhythm.” Ask what could be causing circulatory arrest: hypoxia, hypovolemia, electrolyte/metabolic problems, hypothermia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, thrombosis, toxins and other treatable causes.

5. Special situations

  • Pregnancy ≥20 weeks: early transport consideration, high-quality CPR, defibrillation when indicated, and manual uterine displacement.
  • Suspected opioid-related arrest: the lecture highlights uncertain evidence for naloxone once cardiac arrest is established; standard resuscitation priorities remain central.
  • DNR: a valid DNR directs resuscitation decisions in VSA; it is not the same thing as “do not treat.”

6. TOR

Termination of resuscitation depends on the current medical directive and the specific clinical situation. Use the ALS PCS and Companion Document for the exact criteria and wording. Do not learn TOR from a one-line summary alone.

RECALL • Cardiac arrest checks
Which cardiac-arrest rhythms are shockable?
Pulseless ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.
Which are non-shockable?
Asystole and pulseless electrical activity (PEA).
What is PEA?
Organized electrical activity on the monitor without a palpable pulse. It is a clinical condition, not a single ECG rhythm.
Does a DNR mean “do not treat”?
No. DNR concerns resuscitation in VSA; it does not automatically prohibit other indicated care.
Where should you learn exact TOR criteria?
The current ALS PCS / applicable medical directive and Companion Document.

LEARN • Week 2 high-yield framework

1. Trauma priorities before the medication

The lecture’s trauma message is rapid movement toward definitive trauma care. For major or multisystem trauma, scene treatment should focus on interventions that materially change immediate survival, including catastrophic hemorrhage control and management of pneumothorax/tension pneumothorax.

Anchor idea: do not let TXA delay trauma management or transport. The lecture repeatedly puts hemorrhage control, reversible-cause management and rapid transport ahead of stopping on scene for TXA.

2. Lethal diamond of death

AcidosisBlood loss → reduced tissue oxygen delivery → anaerobic metabolism → lactate/metabolic acidosis, which further impairs cellular and clotting function.
HypothermiaBlood loss, exposure and impaired thermoregulation reduce temperature and impair clotting enzymes/platelets. The lecture states clotting decreases about 10% per 1°C drop.
CoagulopathyBlood loss, dilution and hypothermia impair clot formation, perpetuating hemorrhage.
HypocalcemiaReduced ionized calcium can decrease coagulation, cardiac signaling and smooth-muscle contraction.

3. TXA — what the lecture wants you to understand

Tranexamic acid (TXA) is presented as an antifibrinolytic. It blocks lysine-binding sites on plasminogen, interfering with plasminogen binding/conversion and helping protect established fibrin clot from breakdown. The lecture places TXA’s action in the fibrinolysis stage of hemostasis.

  • CRASH-2 in the lecture: 20,127 trauma patients across 274 hospitals in 40 countries.
  • The lecture emphasizes greatest benefit when TXA is given early, especially within 1 hour, with benefit declining after 3 hours.
  • Adverse effects listed include hypotension with rapid administration, thromboembolic events, nausea/vomiting, dizziness, seizures and diarrhea.

4. Traumatic Hemorrhage Medical Directive — lecture table

IndicationSuspected hemorrhage due to trauma AND hemodynamic instability.
ConditionsAge ≥16 years; lecture table lists HR ≥110 BPM or hypotension.
ContraindicationsAllergy/sensitivity to TXA; greater than 3 hours from injury to administration or unknown injury time; isolated head injury.
TreatmentTXA 1000 mg IV or IM; maximum single dose 1000 mg; maximum 1 dose.
  • IV TXA is shown as a slow injection over at least 5 minutes; rapid administration can cause hypotension.
  • The lecture notes TXA is supplied as 1000 mg/10 mL. It lists a maximum 5 mL IM volume per vastus lateralis and using both vastus lateralis sites for the full IM dose.
  • TXA can be considered for internal traumatic bleeding, not only visibly external bleeding.
  • The lecture specifically states not to delay transport to obtain IV access/administer TXA; it can be done en route.

5. Traumatic Cardiac Arrest

The lecture directive identifies cardiac arrest secondary to severe blunt or penetrating trauma. CPR is performed in 2-minute intervals. Manual defibrillation is considered for VF or pulseless VT when available and authorized.

Trauma VSA priority: the lecture emphasizes correcting reversible causes such as uncontrolled hemorrhage, tension pneumothorax, asphyxia and cardiac tamponade. It explicitly says TXA is not part of the current traumatic-arrest algorithm presented in class.

6. Trauma TOR

The Trauma TOR slide is exact-wording material. It includes age ≥16, no palpable pulse, no defibrillation delivered and an asystole/PEA pathway tied to signs of life, full extrication and transport time. It is shown as a Mandatory Provincial Patch Point. Use the current ALS PCS/Companion for the exact operational wording.

7. ROSC

OxygenationLecture target SpO₂ 94–98%.
VentilationAvoid hyperventilation; target ETCO₂ 30–40 mmHg with continuous waveform capnography if available.
FluidConsider 0.9% NaCl 10 mL/kg for hypotension when chest auscultation is clear; maximum 1000 mL.
ECGConsider 12-lead ECG acquisition and interpretation after ROSC.
  • For ages ≥2 to <12 years, the lecture table reassesses the bolus every 100 mL.
  • For age ≥12 years, reassess every 250 mL.
  • The lecture’s MAP slide lists adults target MAP ≥65 mmHg and pediatrics target MAP ≥55 mmHg.
ROSC sequence: note time of ROSC, repeat primary survey, establish monitoring, address ventilation/oxygenation, reassess hemodynamics, obtain 12-lead ECG and continue reassessment/transport planning.

What four problems make up the trauma lethal diamond?
Acidosis, hypothermia, coagulopathy and hypocalcemia.
What does TXA do at a high level?
It is an antifibrinolytic that helps stabilize formed fibrin clot by interfering with plasminogen/plasmin-mediated fibrinolysis.
What are the lecture’s three major TXA contraindication groups?
Allergy/sensitivity to TXA; administration more than 3 hours after injury or unknown injury time; isolated head injury.
What is the Week 2 TXA dose shown for PCPs?
1000 mg IV or IM, one dose.
Which traumatic-arrest rhythms are candidates for manual defibrillation?
VF or pulseless VT.
ROSC oxygenation and ETCO₂ targets in the lecture?
SpO₂ 94–98% and ETCO₂ 30–40 mmHg.
ROSC fluid bolus highlighted in the lecture?
0.9% NaCl 10 mL/kg for hypotension when the chest is clear, to a maximum of 1000 mL.
