LEARN • SGA directive review

What this lecture wants you to know

The lecture introduces the Supraglottic Airway Medical Directive, the I-GEL device, ALS PCS wording and the Companion Document. The exam-relevant part is not merely “what is an SGA?” It is knowing the directive structure and the wording that controls when/how it is used.

PurposeAn SGA provides a ventilation route above the glottic opening when a patient cannot adequately maintain/protect the airway.
I-GELThe Week 1 lecture uses the I-GEL as the primary device example.
Attempt limitThe lecture highlights a maximum of 2 attempts. Confirm exact wording in the current ALS PCS/Companion Document.
ConfirmationPlacement must be confirmed using the methods required by the standards; effective ventilation and capnography are key clinical tools.

Ventilation after an advanced airway

  • Chest compressions can continue continuously once an advanced airway is appropriately established in cardiac arrest.
  • The lecture lists adults at approximately 1 breath every 6 seconds.
  • The lecture lists pediatrics at approximately 1 breath every 3 seconds.
  • Avoid excessive ventilation; deliver enough volume to produce visible chest rise.
Test strategy: exact wording, contraindications, attempt limits, required confirmation and considerations are high-yield. Read the actual directive alongside this explanation.

It contributes to confirmation/ongoing assessment of ventilation and can help detect displacement or changes in perfusion during resuscitation.

About 1 breath every 6 seconds.

LEARN • Cardiac arrest framework

1. Separate electrical rhythm from pulse status

In arrest care, the monitor does not tell you everything. An organized-looking rhythm may still produce no pulse.

Ventricular fibrillationChaotic ventricular electrical activity with no effective organized ventricular contraction. In cardiac arrest, VF is shockable.
Pulseless VTVentricular tachycardia with no pulse. In cardiac arrest, pulseless VT is shockable.
AsystoleAbsence of meaningful ventricular electrical activity. Non-shockable.
PEAOrganized electrical activity without a palpable pulse. It is a clinical state, not one specific ECG pattern. Non-shockable.
Simple arrest rule: pulseless VF/VT = shockable. Asystole + PEA = non-shockable. VT with a pulse is not cardiac arrest and is managed differently.

2. CPR and ventilation updates highlighted in class

  • Ventilate only until visible chest rise; each breath is delivered over about 1 second.
  • Avoid hyperventilation because it can worsen hemodynamics and increase gastric insufflation/aspiration risk.
  • The lecture emphasizes a two-handed mask seal with one rescuer focused on the airway where staffing allows.
  • The lecture notes updated preferred infant compression techniques instead of the traditional two-finger method.
  • For choking in adults/children, the lecture notes alternating back blows and abdominal thrusts.

3. Pediatric symptomatic bradycardia

The lecture emphasizes HR < 60/min with cardiopulmonary compromise, rapid assessment of the cause, and optimization of airway, oxygenation and ventilation. If major bradycardia with compromise persists despite effective oxygenation/ventilation, CPR is initiated.

4. Reversible causes

Do not memorize PEA as “just a rhythm.” Ask what could be causing circulatory arrest: hypoxia, hypovolemia, electrolyte/metabolic problems, hypothermia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, thrombosis, toxins and other treatable causes.

5. Special situations

  • Pregnancy ≥20 weeks: early transport consideration, high-quality CPR, defibrillation when indicated, and manual uterine displacement.
  • Suspected opioid-related arrest: the lecture highlights uncertain evidence for naloxone once cardiac arrest is established; standard resuscitation priorities remain central.
  • DNR: a valid DNR directs resuscitation decisions in VSA; it is not the same thing as “do not treat.”

6. TOR

Termination of resuscitation depends on the current medical directive and the specific clinical situation. Use the ALS PCS and Companion Document for the exact criteria and wording. Do not learn TOR from a one-line summary alone.

RECALL • Cardiac arrest checks
Which cardiac-arrest rhythms are shockable?
Pulseless ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.
Which are non-shockable?
Asystole and pulseless electrical activity (PEA).
What is PEA?
Organized electrical activity on the monitor without a palpable pulse. It is a clinical condition, not a single ECG rhythm.
Does a DNR mean “do not treat”?
No. DNR concerns resuscitation in VSA; it does not automatically prohibit other indicated care.
Where should you learn exact TOR criteria?
The current ALS PCS / applicable medical directive and Companion Document.
